AKARI (formerly ASTRO-F) is the Japanese satellite developed by the Institute of Space and Astronautical Science (ISAS) and the Japanese Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA). It is an infrared astronomy satellite whose main objective is to perform an all-sky survey. AKARI is a successor to IRAS, possessing higher spatial resolution and wider wavelength coverage. It is capable of mapping the entire sky in six infrared bands from 9 to 180 µm.
AKARI operated with a 68.5 cm-diameter telescope cooled to 6 K. Its observational wavelength ranges between 2 and 180 µm. The satellite follows a sun-synchronous polar orbit at 700 km altitude. It was launched on 21 February 2006, by an M-V rocket from the Uchinoura Space Center (USC), Japan. 
AKARI started its mission on 8 May 2006 and operated undisturbed until 26 August 2007, after which its onboard supply of liquid helium coolant ran out. The satellite achieved its expected lifetime of 550 days, during which more than 94% of the sky was covered in survey mode.
During its mission, AKARI  performed more than 5,000 pointed observations in 13 bands. It provided a comprehensive multi-wavelength, photometric and spectroscopic coverage of a variety of astronomical objects. These included nearby Solar System bodies, zodiacal light, brown dwarfs, young stars, debris disks and evolved stars in the Milky Way Galaxy and in other galaxies of the Local Group. Further achievements involved probing galaxy evolution and the large scale structure of the Universe out to great distances. It also provided an extensive and dedicated coverage of two high-visibility regions, the Large Magellanic Cloud and the North Ecliptic Polar Region.
Currently, AKARI is in its Post-Helium phase. Its imaging and spectroscopic capabilities cover a wavelength range from 1.8 to 5.5 µm. 
An Announcement of Opportunity (AO) for Open Time observations for the first year of this phase opened on 12 May 2008 and closed on 4 July 2008. The approved observing programmes for this Call began on 15 October 2008. An AO for the following year opened on 27 May 2009 and closed on 6 July. The approved programmes from this AO have started on 15 October 2009.
JAXA/ISAS, the developers of AKARI, collaborate with ESA in order to increase the scientific output of the mission. ESA, providing tracking support from its ground station in Kiruna, helps in capturing larger amounts of data and accelerating the production of sky catalogues. These catalogues in turn provide vital data for the Herschel and Planck missions. 
In return, 10% of the observing opportunities in the non-survey parts of the mission is distributed to the European scientific bodies through the means of Calls for Proposals, followed by peer reviews. European Space Astronomy Centre (ESAC) in Madrid then provides the attitude reconstruction for the survey data and user support for pointed observations via online sources. 
The AKARI mission is supported by an international consortium, including Imperial College University of London, Open University, University of Kent, Sussex University, University of Groningen, Groningen Campus of SRON and Seoul National University. These bodies collaborate in the data reduction of the All-Sky Survey undertaken by AKARI.
